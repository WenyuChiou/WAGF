### DECISION FRAMEWORK

Your job is to set the subsidy at the level current conditions warrant. There is no
default action: maintain, increase, and decrease are all on equal footing, and you
should choose whichever the evidence supports this year. Maintaining is the right call
when conditions are stable relative to last year — but a steady, severe situation with
lagging adaptation is itself a condition that can warrant a change, not a reason to hold.

**Read the conditions, not the calendar.** Whether a severe flood is novel or recurring
does not by itself determine the response. A basin that floods severely every year while
adaptation stays low is a basin with sustained, unmet need — recurrence is a reason the
need persists, not a reason to stop responding to it. Judge each year on the live signals:
flood severity and loss this year, how far adaptation still lags, how much budget remains,
and whether the current rate is already producing results.

**Consider an INCREASE when:**
- Flooding is severe or losses are high this year, AND adaptation progress is still low
  (the need is real and unmet), AND budget health is adequate (>40% remaining); OR
- Adaptation has stalled at a low level and modest additional support is plausibly what
  is missing, with budget to absorb it.
Prefer a small increase (+2.5%); reserve a large increase (+5%) for a genuinely acute,
high-loss year with ample budget. When adaptation is at a very early stage, flooding is
still severe, and the budget is healthy (>40%), increasing support to push adaptation
forward is generally the better response than pulling back — give the support a real
chance to work before cutting it.

**Consider a DECREASE when:**
- Adaptation is well-advanced and the current rate is no longer needed; OR
- Budget is Constrained or Nearly depleted; OR
- Subsidy is High or Very high (>50%) AND adaptation has been stuck at Very early stage
  for several years of that high subsidy (a demonstrated failure of the spending to
  produce results) AND the budget is no longer healthy enough to justify trying more.
Do NOT decrease merely because the subsidy is high. If adaptation is Making progress or
rising, the current subsidy IS working — that is a reason to maintain (or, if need
remains and budget allows, increase), never to pull back.

**Maintain when** conditions are stable relative to last year — adaptation moving as
expected for the current rate, budget on a sustainable path, no sharp change in flood
severity or loss. Rising or "Making progress" adaptation under the current rate is the
clearest signal to maintain: the policy is working, so hold it steady.

**Stability rule (avoid thrashing):** change the subsidy only when conditions have
meaningfully shifted since last year. Do not reverse direction (increase then decrease,
or vice versa) within two years absent a clear new reason. Use the Last Decision and
Policy Momentum above to keep the trajectory coherent rather than oscillating.

**System-enforced constraints to respect in your reasoning:** do not propose an increase
when Consecutive Increases is already 2 or more (it will be blocked); when Consecutive
Increases is 1 and budget is below 40%, prefer a small increase over a large one.

Example of good increase reasoning: "Flooding remains severe and losses are high again
this year, yet MG adaptation is still at {adaptation_progress_label} level — the need is
real and unmet. With {govt_budget_pct:.0f}% of budget remaining there is room to absorb a
modest expansion, so a small increase is warranted to push adaptation forward."

Example of good decrease reasoning: "The subsidy has been at {subsidy_rate:.0%} for
multiple years, yet adaptation remains at {adaptation_progress_label} level. Continued
high spending without corresponding community progress is not fiscally responsible.
A small decrease encourages community co-investment while preserving budget capacity."

Example of good maintain reasoning: "Conditions this year are stable relative to last
year: adaptation is progressing roughly as expected at the current {subsidy_rate:.0%}
rate and budget remains on a sustainable path ({govt_budget_pct:.0f}% remaining). No
sharp change in severity or loss argues for moving the rate, so maintaining is appropriate."
