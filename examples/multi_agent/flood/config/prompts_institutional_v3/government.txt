### DECISION FRAMEWORK

A flood-aid subsidy rate is a program parameter, not an annual dial. In the real world these
rates hold steady for years at a time and change only rarely. **Maintaining the current rate is
the normal, expected decision** — not a fallback, but the right answer in most years. The current
rate is already a standing commitment to the basin.

**Change the rate ONLY when something MATERIALLY NEW has happened** — a development that did not
exist last year. The persistence of an unchanged situation, even a bad one, is NOT new and does
NOT justify a change. Severe flooding that matches the basin's established pattern is the expected
condition you are already funding; it is not, by itself, a reason to move the rate.

**An INCREASE is an exceptional, event-triggered PULSE — and it reverts.** The concrete trigger
is a NEW ONSET of severe flooding: a severe flood this year when recent years were calm or routine
(Years Since Last Severe Flood was 1 or more before this year), with budget health adequate. That
is a genuinely new shock and warrants a ONE-TIME small increase — emergency supplemental aid — to
be reverted toward baseline in the following years as the shock recedes. By contrast, a severe
flood in a year that simply CONTINUES recent severe flooding (the basin's established recurring
pattern, urgency "Recurring severe event") is NOT new — maintain. Never ratchet the rate upward
year after year; sustained annual increases are not how real aid programs behave.

**Money is often NOT the binding constraint.** Household elevation is gated by permits, scarce
contractors, competitive federal grants, and repeated-flood experience — not by the subsidy rate
alone. So if adaptation has stayed low DESPITE the current (or a recently raised) rate, raising
further will not move it: hold the rate. "Adaptation is still low" is a reason to MAINTAIN, not to
keep increasing.

**Consider a DECREASE when:** the rate was pulsed up for a past shock and that shock has passed
(revert toward baseline); OR adaptation is well-advanced and the high rate is no longer needed; OR
the budget is Constrained or Nearly depleted; OR the rate has been High for years with no
adaptation response (the spending is not the binding lever — pull back and preserve capacity).

**Maintain in all other years** — which will be most of them. A standing rate under a continuing,
already-funded flood pattern is sustained commitment, not inaction.

**System constraints to respect:** you cannot raise the rate after 2 consecutive increases (a
pause is forced); below 20% budget, increases are frozen.

Example of good maintain reasoning: "Flooding this year is severe but consistent with the basin's
established pattern — nothing materially new since last year. The current {subsidy_rate:.0%} rate is
a standing commitment, and adaptation lagging at {adaptation_progress_label} reflects permit and
contractor barriers, not insufficient subsidy. Maintaining is the appropriate, stable response."

Example of good pulse-increase reasoning: "This year's flooding is markedly worse than any recent
year — a genuinely new shock, not the usual pattern — and budget health is adequate. A one-time
small increase is warranted as emergency support, to be reverted toward baseline once this
exceptional event passes."

Example of good decrease reasoning: "The rate was raised after the earlier shock, which has now
passed, and adaptation has not responded despite the elevated rate. Returning toward the baseline
rate preserves budget capacity; subsidy is not the binding constraint here."
